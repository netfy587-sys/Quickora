About the evening

The concert brings together a small ensemble for an unamplified set built around strings, piano, and voice. The room is kept intimate on purpose — no more than a few hundred seats — so the sound stays close and the pauses between pieces are part of the listening, not a gap to fill.

The first half moves through quieter, older pieces; the second half opens up into newer, more rhythmic material written for this specific lineup. There is no interval chatter or introduction between every piece — the musicians let the programme speak for itself.

The idea behind the evening is simple: give a small room enough quiet that the music does not have to compete with anything.

The hall

The Old Chapel Hall was built in the early twentieth century and used as a place of worship for several decades before it was converted into a public hall. The original wooden roof beams and the tall arched windows were kept during the renovation, which is part of why the room carries sound the way it does — long, warm, and without the flatness of a purpose-built auditorium.

Because the space was never designed for amplified sound, the hall has stayed a favourite for chamber and acoustic performances over the years. The seating is arranged in a shallow curve facing the old altar platform, which now serves as the stage. There is no raised seating at the back, so the room feels close no matter where you sit.

Outside the main hall is a smaller stone courtyard, used for tea before the concert and again during the pause. It's a good place to arrive early if you'd rather sit outside for a few minutes before finding your seat.

Who is playing

The ensemble is led by a violinist and pianist who have performed together for close to a decade, joined for this programme by a cellist and a vocalist. Each has a background in both classical training and contemporary composition, which shapes how the set moves between the traditional and the new.

On violin

Also arranges most of the ensemble's programmes

Trained originally in classical performance, spent several years playing in chamber groups before starting to write original material for small ensembles. Prefers rooms like this one — untreated, a little imperfect, built for sound rather than for recording.

On piano

Has performed with the violinist since their student years

Studied composition alongside performance, and often reworks older pieces for the specific instruments in a given programme rather than playing them exactly as written.

On cello

Joined the group for this programme

Comes from an orchestral background but has spent the last few years focused on smaller, more intimate settings, drawn to rooms where the instrument doesn't need to compete for space.

Voice

Sings on four of the evening's eight pieces

Trained in both classical and folk traditions, and tends to choose material based on the words rather than the melody first — several of tonight's texts are older poems set to new music.

Before you go

The hall has no strict dress code, though most people dress for a quiet indoor evening. Seating opens half an hour before the music starts, and latecomers are asked to wait for a natural pause before entering. There's a small space outside the hall for tea before and after the concert.

The room stays fairly cool even in warmer months, given the stone walls, so a light layer is worth bringing. Phones are asked to be switched to silent rather than just vibrate, since the room carries even a faint buzz further than you'd expect.

Parking around the hall is limited, and most attendees walk or arrive by rickshaw. If driving, the side street behind the courtyard usually has a little more space than the main road.

Questions people ask

Is there an interval?
Yes, one short pause of about fifteen minutes between the two sets, during which the courtyard is open for tea.
Can children attend?
The evening isn't restricted by age, but the quiet, unamplified nature of the music tends to suit an older audience better.
Is the seating reserved?
No, seating is unreserved. Arriving closer to the doors opening gives a wider choice of seats.
What happens if I arrive late?
Latecomers are seated at the back during a natural pause in the programme rather than mid-piece, so there may be a short wait at the door.
Is photography allowed?
Photography without flash is fine before the music starts and during the pause, but not while a piece is being performed.
